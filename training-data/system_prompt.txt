KUCHEMA — Speculative Photographic Collage

Create a visually rich, speculative photographic collage rooted in the landscapes, communities, ecological knowledge and cultural imaginaries of Chikukwa, Chimanimani, Zimbabwe.

The collage should imagine alternative environmental futures through relationships between water, land, people, plants, animals, ancestral memory and ecological regeneration.

The visual language should resemble physical collages assembled from photographic prints, magazine cuttings and archival material, rather than digital illustrations, paintings or seamless AI-generated scenes.

A. Research and narrative grounding

Use a small, carefully selected collection of local research materials to guide each composition.

Draw from Indigenous ecological knowledge, water cosmologies, community narratives, oral histories, myths, academic research and youth visions of environmental futures.

Translate relevant narratives into specific visual subjects, environments, relationships and transformations.

For example:
- A narrative about water spirits might inspire reflections, submerged landscapes, thresholds between water and land, or unexpected ecological relationships.
- Research about soil conservation might inspire terraced landscapes, regenerating vegetation and relationships between water, soil and agricultural practices.
- Stories about ancestral relationships with sacred environments might inspire layered temporalities, intergenerational gatherings and landscapes where past and future coexist.

Avoid generic representations of African spirituality, invented ceremonial practices or stereotypical fantasy imagery.

Distinguish documented cultural knowledge from artistic speculation. Do not reproduce restricted knowledge or invent sacred identities.

The objective is not to illustrate myths literally, but to allow them to inform the imaginative possibilities of the collage.

B. Human representation

Human presence comes only from direct cutouts of the archive's people photographs, never from generated images. When people are enabled, use at least two distinct people photographs, normally four and at most eight. Crop each whole visible person or group directly from its photograph, preserving original appearance and connected bodies; never isolate a head, face, torso or limb. Never generate, recolour or transform people, and do not place identifiable people in invented rituals, ceremonies, supernatural transformations or traumatic scenes. Use people purposefully within the composition rather than as decorative elements.

C. Photographic and magazine aesthetic

The collage must have the material qualities of a physical photographic montage.

Use:
- Realistic photographic textures and lighting.
- Visible photographic grain and printed-paper surfaces.
- Torn, cut and irregular magazine edges.
- Layered photographic fragments.
- Purposeful overlaps and changes of scale.
- Contrasting image sizes and perspectives.
- Rich colour, tonal variation and atmospheric depth.

Allow landscapes, figures, plants, water and architectural fragments to interrupt and overlap one another.

Maintain the visual distinction between fragments rather than blending everything into a seamless image.

Avoid painterly textures, illustration, cartoon aesthetics, synthetic-looking surfaces and overly smooth AI imagery.

The final image should feel like a handmade collage constructed from photographs, rather than an AI illustration attempting to imitate collage.

D. Composition and artistic direction

Use one selected collage from the artist's reference archive to inform the composition.

Study its visual principles, including layering, density, scale, colour relationships, fragmentation and spatial arrangement.

Do not copy the reference collage directly.

Encourage compositions that are visually complex, atmospheric and experimental.

Combine:
- Environmental landscapes and water.
- Photographic human figures.
- Vegetation, animals and ecological details.
- Speculative transformations.
- Narrative fragments informed by the research archive.

Avoid repetitive arrangements, rigid grids, excessive symmetry and a single dominant background image.

Use a rich, dark environmental ground beneath the fragments where appropriate, avoiding large empty white areas.

The composition should feel like a world assembled from fragments of memory, ecological knowledge and speculative imagination.

E. Provenance and cultural accountability

Maintain a record of the sources informing each collage.

Distinguish between:
- Archival photographs.
- Research texts and community narratives.
- Artist-created references.
- AI-generated imagery.

Record which research passages influenced generated elements and the overall composition.

Do not present speculative imagery as documentary evidence or generated people as actual community members.

Respect contributor permissions and restrictions on sensitive cultural knowledge.

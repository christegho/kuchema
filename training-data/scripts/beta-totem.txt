Beta Wabata is a Shona clan of the Beta totem, the flying termite (ishwa). Its men are greeted as Wabata and Dhliwayo, and its women as MaDhliwayo.

The Praise Poem of the Beta Clan (Dhliwayo / Ishwa)
Introduction
The praise poem of the Beta clan, whose totem (mutupo) is also known as Ishwa or Hanzu, and whose praise name (chidawo) is Dhliwayo, celebrates the beauty, character and history of the people belonging to this clan.

BETA / DHLIWAYO / ISHWA
Ah, we rejoice, Ishwa Dhliwayo,
You have overwhelmed us with your greatness.
We honour those of Chimanimani,
Those who came from Mbire.
We honour you, Mutore,
You who sought refuge in Makoni, passing through Mutasa.
We thank you for your deeds.
Ah, we are truly grateful,
Beta Mandemwa, we acknowledge you.
Well done, people of Chikukwa.
Saungweme, those who emerge from the openings of termite mounds,
Surrounded by swarming termites.
Behold the bustling activity of your soldiers,
Building your home,
A home of sweet, sweet sand,
Rivalling the sweetness of honey.
Where you build, the earth itself is your foundation.
Your dwelling is a chamber beneath the ground.
You emerge through the openings when the rains approach.
Take flight when darkness falls.
You emerge in great numbers,
Gathering together in abundance.
We honour you, Ishwa.
We honour you, Dhliwayo.
Flight is your way of life.
You refuse the helpless fluttering of a bird caught in a snare.
You whose wings are not easily folded against your back.
We honour those who escape through the openings of the earth.
When weary, you respond with your bellies,
Carrying your swollen abdomens.
Beta, we honour you again and again.
Ishwa, we honour you.
You have brought us the rains of Matonjeni.
Today, everything resounds with celebration.
May your wings spread wide.
Fly!
Fly!
Ah, Beta, we acknowledge you.
Ishwa, we honour you.
We rejoice in all that you have done.

Cultural and linguistic notes
This nhetembo is particularly rich in references to ancestral migration, termite ecology, collective labour, seasonal rainfall and relationships between people and the natural world.

A few passages deserve closer attention.

1. Ishwa — The Winged Termite
Ishwa refers to winged termites, particularly those that emerge from termite mounds during the rainy season.
The poem celebrates their distinctive behaviour: living underground, constructing elaborate dwellings, emerging in large numbers and taking flight.
The termite becomes an expression of collective identity, ingenuity and belonging.

2. Chimanimani, Mbire, Makoni and Mutasa
Maita vari Chimanimani
Vakabve Mbire
Makapotera Makoni muchidarika Mutasa

We honour those of Chimanimani,
Those who came from Mbire,
You who sought refuge in Makoni, passing through Mutasa.

These lines preserve a geographical narrative of migration and settlement.
They associate the Beta clan with Mbire and the eastern Zimbabwean landscapes of Makoni, Mutasa and Chimanimani.
The poem itself does not establish when these movements occurred, but it suggests that ancestral migration forms part of the clan's remembered history.

3. Chikukwa - Ancestral Geography
Zvaitwa Chikukwa

Well done, people of Chikukwa.

It places the community within the clan's ancestral geography and suggests a relationship between identity, place and oral tradition.

4. The Termite Mound as a Dwelling
Saungweme, vanonyuka mumwena wechuru
Vakakombwa nemajuru

Saungweme, those who emerge from the openings of termite mounds,
Surrounded by termites.

The imagery of underground chambers, collective construction and emerging from the earth is central to the poem.
It celebrates the termite's relationship with the soil, its architectural abilities and its collective organisation.

5. Matonjeni — Rain and Sacred Geography
Matiunzira mvura yaMatonjeni

You have brought us the rains of Matonjeni.

Matonjeni is associated with the sacred Mwari religious tradition centred in the Matobo Hills, historically connected to rainmaking and spiritual authority.

The reference brings together the seasonal emergence of winged termites and the arrival of rain.

This is especially significant because the emergence of ishwa is closely associated with the rainy season in Zimbabwe.

Within the poem, rain is not simply a meteorological event. It is celebrated in relation to ancestral identity, seasonal renewal and the living environment.

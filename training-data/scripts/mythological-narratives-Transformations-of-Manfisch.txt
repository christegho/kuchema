Shona Mythological Narratives in Transformations of a Manfisch

Five distinct mythological narratives or narrative traditions in the paper are identified in the paper.

1. The creation of Njuzu: God's children sent to live in water
Source: Hans Aschwanden, Karanga Mythology (1989), p. 136
Discussed in: Lilford (1999), p. 201

This is one of the clearest creation stories included in the paper.

The Story
According to the narrative, God speaks to two of his children who possess no wealth (pfuma).
God asks how they intend to acquire the resources necessary to help others.
When they cannot answer, God instructs them to inhabit a large pool.
They are told to capture people who come to collect water and hold them until their relatives provide cattle and glass beads as payment.
However, the captured individuals must not return empty-handed.
Before releasing them, the water beings must give them healing spirits (shave) and medicinal knowledge, enabling them to become traditional healers (n'anga).
This is how the water beings come into existence.

Cosmological Significance

This narrative establishes several relationships:
- Njuzu originate through divine instruction.
- Water becomes a place where humans encounter spiritual knowledge.
- Disappearance into water can result in transformation.
- Healing knowledge originates partly in relationships with water beings.
- The relationship between humans and njuzu involves exchange and obligation.

The important distinction is that abduction is not necessarily presented as an act of destruction. It can be part of a process through which spiritual and medicinal knowledge enters human society.

2. The creation of the world from an egg: Samututu and the water people
Source: Hans Aschwanden, Karanga Mythology (1989), pp. 21, 188
Discussed in: Lilford (1999), p. 206

This is perhaps the most significant creation narrative in the paper.

The Story
In the beginning, there was no land.
There were no mountains, forests or human beings living on the Earth.
Only the people who inhabited water existed.
A creator named Samututu brought the world into existence by breaking an egg.
From this egg, the physical landscape emerged.
Land, mountains, forests and eventually people came into existence.
Following the creation of land and its inhabitants, conflict developed between the water people and the newly created land people.
The water people became jealous of those living on land.

Cosmological Significance
This narrative presents an understanding of creation in which water beings predate terrestrial existence.
The world is not initially an empty landscape awaiting human occupation.
Instead, water already contains life and inhabitants before the emergence of land.

The story establishes a distinction between:
Vagari vomumvura, those who dwell in water and Vagari venyika, those who dwell on land.
It also raises questions about who originally inhabited the world.

Lilford notes that Aschwanden encountered different accounts of the origins of njuzu.
Some storytellers believed that water beings existed before humanity.
Others associated them with vari pasi, beings who live beneath the ground.
Still others believed that njuzu descended from human beings.

These variations suggest that the origins of water beings are not fixed within a single universally accepted cosmology.
This narrative is particularly interesting because water is presented as a primordial world inhabited by beings before the emergence of terrestrial life.

3. The person taken into the sacred pool: Rules of the underwater world
Source: Hans Aschwanden, Karanga Mythology (1989), pp. 191-192
Discussed in: Lilford (1999), pp. 202-203

This is a traditional narrative concerning what happens when a person is taken into a pool inhabited by njuzu.

The Story
A person is pulled beneath the water into the world of the njuzu. Upon arrival, the individual encounters beings who explain the rules governing life in the pool.

These rules include:
- Eating mud rather than ordinary food.
- Avoiding sleeping on a mat.
- Not entering huts.
- Remaining close to snakes and crocodiles.
- Refusing attractive food offered by beautiful women.

The individual is warned that violating these rules will prevent their return to the human world. Those who disobey must remain with the njuzu permanently.

Mhinganidziro
Aschwanden refers to these instructions as mhinganidziro.
Lilford explains that mhinganidziro are traditionally associated with healing practices.
They consist of prescriptions and prohibitions given by a n'anga to facilitate healing.
The rules governing the underwater world therefore resemble those governing traditional healing.

Cosmological Significance
This narrative suggests that the underwater world operates according to its own laws.
Entering this world requires humans to abandon familiar expectations about food, shelter and behaviour.
Snakes and crocodiles are particularly important because they appear as beings with whom the visitor must maintain proximity.
The underwater world is not simply an alternative geographical location.
It is a space of initiation, discipline, transformation and potentially irreversible transition.

4. The disappearance of Njuzu and the drying of rivers
Source: Hans Aschwanden, Karanga Mythology (1989), p. 189
Discussed in: Lilford (1999), pp. 209-210

This is less a single story than a collection of traditional explanations for the disappearance of water beings and the drying of water sources.

The Narrative Tradition
According to the traditions Aschwanden recorded, njuzu inhabit pools, wetlands and springs that do not ordinarily dry out.
They are responsible for maintaining these water sources.
They also act as guardians of rivers.
Historically, many more pools and springs were believed to be inhabited by water beings and water snakes.
However, the arrival of Europeans introduced unfamiliar disturbances, particularly noise.
These disturbances caused some njuzu to abandon their habitats.
When the water beings departed, the water sources dried up.
The traditions also describe njuzu withdrawing from particular places because humans failed to observe the appropriate rules governing sacred waters.

Cosmological Significance
This narrative presents a relationship between the presence of water beings and the continued existence of water.
Water does not simply sustain the njuzu; the njuzu also sustain water.
The relationship is reciprocal.
When humans disturb these beings or violate the conditions governing sacred environments, the beings may withdraw.
Their departure results in ecological degradation.
This narratie connects environmental degradation with the breakdown of relationships between humans and other-than-human beings.

5. A literary adaptation: Fungai's initiation by Njuzu
Source: Christopher Tagwireyi, Dziva reNjuzu (1980), p. 22
Discussed in: Lilford (1999), pp. 202-203

Although this is not presented as a directly collected oral narrative, it is one of the most detailed accounts of an encounter with njuzu in the paper.


The Story
A young girl named Fungai disappears after being taken by a njuzu.
She is brought into the water being's dwelling, described as a cave.
Inside, she encounters a female being who speaks an unfamiliar language.
The being takes her into a room containing maggots and other unpleasant things.
Fungai is offered two plates of food.
One contains maggots.
The other contains watery mud.
Although frightened and disgusted, Fungai eats what she is given.
The female njuzu smiles.
For several days, Fungai continues eating the same food.
After approximately one month, the njuzu begins feeding her fish and crabs.
Eventually, Fungai returns to her community.
She explains that she was undergoing a form of initiation into traditional healing.
Those who reject the food offered by the njuzu are forbidden from returning to their families.

Cosmological Significance
This narrative contains several important elements:
Water as a place of initiation: Fungai enters the underwater world and emerges with new knowledge.
Food as a test: The ability to accept unfamiliar and unpleasant substances demonstrates humility and discipline.
Spiritual law: The njuzu operate according to rules that humans must respect.
Transformation: Fungai's experience prepares her to become a traditional healer.
Female spiritual authority: The njuzu appears as a female being who controls Fungai's initiation.
This is a particularly rich example of water as a place where knowledge is transmitted through embodied experience rather than verbal instruction alone.

6. A literary account: The sacred pool that dries up
Source: Christopher Tagwireyi, Dziva reNjuzu (1980), p. 19
Discussed in: Lilford (1999), p. 210

Tagwireyi also describes a prohibition against bringing a blackened cooking pot to a pool inhabited by njuzu.
According to the account, someone who approaches the pool with a soot-covered pot may discover the following morning that the entire pool has dried up.
The njuzu are said to dislike blackened objects and may abandon the water source.
Lilford interprets this prohibition as relating primarily to ritual cleanliness and purity, rather than colour itself.
He notes that njuzu may accept a completely black cow as an offering, suggesting that the objection concerns contamination rather than blackness.
The account reinforces the idea that water beings regulate human behaviour around sacred water sources.

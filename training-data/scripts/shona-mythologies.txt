# Zimbabwean Water Mythologies: Extracts from the Two Papers

Focusing specifically on mythological narratives, cosmological beliefs, ancestral relationships with water, rainmaking traditions and ritual practices.

The two papers offer different kinds of material:

1. Carin Vijfhuizen (1997), Rain-Making, Political Conflicts and Gender Images: A Case from Mutema Chieftaincy in Zimbabwe.

It documents several Ndau oral narratives from Chipinge, including the origins of rainmaking powers, ancestors who control rainfall, sacred places and women's roles in transmitting spiritual knowledge.

2. Takudzwa Musekiwa (2025), Shona Oral Traditions and the Quest for Cognitive Justice.

This provides a broader cosmological framework, particularly concerning Dzivaguru, the creation of rivers, Mwari/ Musikavanhu and the relationship between water and spiritual authority.

Below, are the actual stories:

---

# PART I - NDAU WATER MYTHOLOGIES
### Carin Vijfhuizen (1997)

## 1. The chief who cut open the clouds to make rain

Pages 36-37 | Original narrative recorded from a Mutema traditional leader.

### The Story

A chief named Saurozvi, also called Chikomba Charipirwa, becomes involved in a conflict with his brother.

His brother drives him away from his territory.

Following his departure, the rains stop.

The community experiences drought and eventually asks Saurozvi to return.

Upon his return, dark clouds gather in the sky.

Saurozvi takes a large sword, raises it toward the heavens and cuts open the clouds.

Rain begins to fall.

According to this account, the chief subsequently receives the name Mutema, derived from kutema (to cut), because of his ability to cut the clouds and release rain.

### Cosmological Significance

The narrative establishes a relationship between legitimate leadership, ancestral authority and the material behaviour of the sky.

The chief's absence results in drought, while his return restores rainfall.

The sword becomes an instrument through which spiritual authority is expressed.

Water is therefore not independent of social relationships. Rainfall responds to the restoration of rightful leadership.

This is a particularly compelling example of a ritual object participating in a cosmological relationship.

---

## 2. The chief whose return brought rain after years of drought

Page 37 | Alternative oral narrative recorded from Semwayo, another explanation for the origins of the name Mutema.

### The Story

According to Semwayo, the chief Munodani was enthroned at Madzadza.

His younger brother, Muraaswo, wanted to overthrow him and take control of the chieftaincy.

Munodani fled.

During his absence, the territory experienced drought for approximately seven to ten years.

The community eventually decided to bring him back.

When Munodani returned to Madzadza, the rains began.

In this version, Mutema is associated with the sound of rain striking a roof:

Tema, tema, tema.

Semwayo interprets Mutema as meaning the establisher of peace.

### Cosmological Significance

This version differs from the first.

Rain does not return because the chief physically cuts open the clouds.

Instead, rainfall accompanies the restoration of political and spiritual order.

The two stories preserve different explanations for the same chiefly title.

The important cosmological relationship is that rain and peace are interconnected.

---

## 3. The woman who carried the secrets of rainmaking

Pages 37-38 | Oral narrative recounted by Semwayo

### The Story

Shiriyedenga, the founding chief of Mutema, marries a woman from the Musikavanhu chieftaincy.

During their marriage, the woman learns the secrets of rainmaking.

She acquires knowledge of the mushonga (medicine) associated with bringing rain.

When conflict develops between the couple, she leaves her husband and returns to her own people.

She takes the rainmaking knowledge with her.

As a result, the Musikavanhu people acquire knowledge that, according to this version, originally belonged to Mutema.

However, the Mutema people retain some of their own knowledge.

Neither chieftaincy can now successfully conduct rainmaking independently.

They must cooperate.

### Cosmological Significance

This narrative presents rainmaking knowledge as something that can be learned, carried, transferred and shared between communities.

Knowledge is not simply attached to a particular territory or inherited exclusively through male lineage.

A woman becomes the agent through whom sacred knowledge moves.

The story also establishes an important principle:

The power to bring rain depends on relationships between different knowledge holders.

## 4. Nyakuvimba and Chapo: The origins of Musikavanhu rainmaking

Page 44 | Historical oral tradition documented by J.K. Rennie (1973)

A different narrative concerning the origins of Musikavanhu's rainmaking authority.

### The Story

According to the account documented by Rennie, Nyakuvimba, the younger brother of Shiriyedenga, establishes the Musikavanhu chieftaincy.

Nyakuvimba and his sister Chapo obtain ritual objects associated with bringing rain from a Rozvi king.

The objects are described as having been stolen.

Nyakuvimba subsequently flees but is killed.

However, the rainmaking medicine survives.

It is passed from one successor to another, allowing the Musikavanhu chieftaincy to maintain its rainmaking authority across generations.

In this version, Musikavanhu is not dependent on having acquired knowledge through a woman married into Mutema.

Instead, the chieftaincy possesses its own origin narrative concerning the acquisition and inheritance of rainmaking power.

### Cosmological Significance

The narrative establishes several relationships:

Rainmaking power is embodied in material objects.

The objects carry knowledge and authority that can survive the death of an individual.

Women participate in acquiring sacred knowledge.

Chapo is not a passive figure. She participates directly in obtaining the objects.

Spiritual knowledge is transmitted through succession.

Rainmaking authority is maintained through intergenerational custodianship.

The precise nature of the ritual objects is not disclosed in the account. They might have included cloth, a sword or clay pots.

## 5. Shiriyedenga: The flying ancestor and the sacred site of Madzadza

Page 36 | Oral account recorded from Mutema traditional leaders

### The Story

Shiriyedenga, the first chief of Mutema, was believed to possess the ability to fly.

He was also called Chiphaphami, a name associated with the sound of his wings:

Pha-pha-pha.

According to the account, when Shiriyedenga attended gatherings, he would tell others to leave before him.

Although he departed last, he would arrive at the next gathering before everyone else.

His ability to fly established his extraordinary spiritual authority.

The sacred site of Madzadza is associated with the place where Shiriyedenga first descended to establish his chieftaincy.

The name Madzadza is connected to kudzadza, meaning to fulfil or satisfy people's needs.

The site became a place for communicating with royal ancestors, requesting rainfall, resolving disputes and enthroning chiefs.

### Cosmological Significance

This narrative connects the sky, ancestral authority, sacred geography and rainfall.

Madzadza is not simply a geographical location. It is understood as a place where an ancestral figure descended and established a relationship between spiritual authority and the land.

The story is not specifically about water, but its connection to Madzadza makes it relevant to the wider rainmaking cosmology

## 6. The hippo and the cattle: Totemic relationships with water knowledge

Page 38 | Explanation recorded from community members

An interesting explanation for why the Musikavanhu chieftaincy possesses particular knowledge of rainmaking.

The two chieftaincies have different totems:

- Musikavanhu: Muyambo/Mvuu - hippopotamus.
- Mutema: Mombe - cattle.

One informant explains that because hippopotamuses live in water, Musikavanhu possesses greater knowledge of water.

Cattle, by contrast, live on land.

Consequently, Mutema is understood as having a different relationship with water.

### Cosmological Significance

This account suggests that totemic relationships can inform the kinds of ecological and spiritual knowledge associated with particular communities.

Knowledge of water is connected to kinship with an aquatic animal. It suggests that relationships between humans and animals can also be relationships between different forms of environmental knowledge.

It is important to distinguish this from a complete mythological story. It as a community explanation for rainmaking authority.

---

## 7. The ancestors who withheld rain because communities were divided

Pages 40-43 | Contemporary accounts of ancestral intervention

This is not a single origin myth but a collection of accounts documenting how ancestral spirits are understood to influence rainfall.

### The Accounts

The belief that royal ancestral spirits (vadzimu) can bring rain or withhold it.

When traditional leaders quarrel, they may fail to perform the necessary ceremonies together.

The ancestors become displeased, and rainfall may be withheld.

One particularly interesting account concerns a conflict between two leaders over authority in a village.

The ancestors were believed to reside in a sacred forest, where members of the original ruling lineage were buried.

The dispute concerned who possessed the ancestral authority to conduct ceremonies at that place.

The ancestors reportedly instructed the leaders to share their authority and cooperate.

Rain would return once the conflict was resolved.

### The Makopa account

A gathering in November 1995 during which Makopa, a royal ancestral spirit communicating through a female medium, urged the community's leaders to end their divisions.

Makopa described rain clouds gathering in the sky but indicated that rainfall would not arrive until the community restored its relationships and fulfilled its obligations.

### Cosmological Significance

These accounts establish an important relationship:

Social harmony - Ancestral communication - Rainfall - Ecological wellbeing

Rain is understood as responsive to the relationships between communities, ancestors and traditional authorities.

The sacred forest is also significant because ancestral presence is connected to a particular landscape.

---

## 8. Madzadza: Reconciliation and the return of rain

Page 43 | Ritual account documented in January 1996

A dispute between traditional authorities over who should lead ceremonies at Madzadza.

A gathering was organised with the spirit medium of Makopa to resolve the disagreement.

The leaders acknowledged their failures and sought forgiveness.

Beer was brewed as part of the reconciliation process, described as ruregerero.

The following day, rain fell in the Mutema chieftaincy.

Community members interpreted the rainfall as a response to the restoration of harmony.

### Important Shona Concepts

| Term | Meaning in the paper |
|---|---|
| Ruregerero | Reparation or seeking forgiveness |
| Kuregererwa | Being forgiven |
| Kudzadza | Fulfilment or satisfaction of needs |
| Kukumbira mvura | Requesting rain |
| Mudzimu / Vadzimu | Ancestral spirit / ancestral spirits |
| Svikiro | Spirit medium |

### Cosmological Significance

The account connects reconciliation, ritual offerings, ancestral communication and rainfall. It provides an example of ritual action intended to restore relationships between humans, ancestors and the environment.

# PART II - SHONA WATER COSMOLOGIES
### Takudzwa Musekiwa (2025)

The second paper provides fewer complete stories but contains two particularly relevant discussions.

## 9. Dzivaguru and the creation of rivers

Page 6 | Section: Shona Oralities - Histories, Myths and Folktales

Musekiwa refers to a mythological tradition called Dzivaguru and the Creation of Rivers.

### The cosmological account

Dzivaguru is identified as one of the praise names of Mwari, associated with water and the maintenance of rivers and water sources.

The author describes Dzivaguru as a spiritual presence responsible for sustaining water systems.

The tradition is presented as an explanation of the cyclical nature of life and the spiritual relationships between humans and the natural environment.

Rivers and water sources are therefore understood as part of a sacred world that humans have responsibilities toward.

### Cosmological significance

The most important relationship is:

Mwari / Dzivaguru - Rivers and water sources - Life - Human responsibility toward nature

The paper connects this understanding with reciprocity between humans, spiritual beings and the environment.

---

## 10. Matonjeni: The sacred place of communication with Mwari

Pages 5-6 | Shona cosmology and ancestral communication

Musekiwa describes Matonjeni, in southwestern Zimbabwe, as an important sacred place for communication with Mwari.

### The cosmological account

Mwari, also known as Musikavanhu, is understood as the creator of the Earth and its beings.

Although Mwari is omnipresent, communication with the living traditionally occurs through particular spiritual intermediaries and sacred places.

Matonjeni is described as a major site where svikiro conduct ceremonies requesting:

- Rainfall.
- Fertility.
- Guidance concerning community affairs.

The shrine is also associated with ancestral spirits, connecting the spiritual and physical worlds.

### Cosmological significance

Matonjeni functions as a place of communication between different realms of existence.

The relationship can be understood as:

Human communities - Spirit mediums - Sacred shrine - Mwari - Rain and fertility

Unlike the Dzivaguru reference, this is not presented as a creation myth but as an explanation of spiritual geography and ritual communication.

---

## 11. Mwedzi and Masasi: Celestial cycles and the natural world

Page 6 | Shona creation mythology

Although not directly concerned with water, Musekiwa includes a cosmological narrative about Mwedzi (Moon) and Masasi (Stars).

### The story

Mwedzi is represented as a male figure who marries Masasi.

Feeling lonely, he marries additional Masasi.

Eventually, Mwedzi becomes dissatisfied and withdraws into solitude.

The author interprets the narrative as an explanation of lunar cycles and the rhythms of growth and decline in the natural world.

### Cosmological significance

The narrative presents celestial bodies through relationships resembling those of human beings.

It connects human relationships, cosmic movement and natural cycles.

This provides supplementary cosmological context, particularly when considered alongside traditions linking celestial movements with seasonal rainfall.

# PART III - Additional knowledge

## 12. Women as custodians of rainmaking knowledge

One of the strongest findings is the importance of women as spiritual authorities.

There are recollections of women serving as mediums for royal ancestral spirits in both Mutema and Musikavanhu.

For example, Zvavakuru Chanaka serves as the medium of Makopa, the royal ancestor of Mutema.

She communicates ancestral guidance, helps resolve conflicts and participates in preparations for rainmaking.

Women also prepare ritual beer for ancestral ceremonies.

In the practices described, beer preparation is entrusted to older women who have passed menopause and observe particular restrictions.

The paper also documents women communicating directly with female ancestors, known as madziteteguru.

These practices demonstrate that women are not simply assisting male ritual authorities. They can hold significant spiritual and political authority themselves.

## 13. Sacred knowledge and secrecy

In discussing the rainmaking objects supposedly taken from Mutema, the precise nature of the objects remains secret.

The researcher speculates that they may have included cloth, a sword or clay pots, but she cannot establish this.

The secrecy itself is part of the knowledge system.

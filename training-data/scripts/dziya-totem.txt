The Praise Poem of the Shayamombe Dziva Clan
An English translation of the Shona praise poem (nhetembo)

Shayamombe,
Mandinginda, the fish that dwells in the pool,
We honour you, Dziva, Save.
You who are not struck with a cloth covered in dung,
The one who is despised, Nhemahuru.
Zindinginda, the cloud that hangs in the sky,
The one without blemish,
We honour those of Majenya,
We acknowledge those of Nyaurungu.
We honour this one of my Nhobvu,
Who came from Dzokota, bearing the sacred totem of Dziva.
The pool is the fish; the fish is nyamasase.
We give thanks to those who dwell in the water,
The bathing place of young women.
Greetings, Muzivikani, my precious ornament.
Greetings, Mironga, Hunga, Dedza,
We recognise the people of Mudziva, of Murambatsvina.
We honour those of Goto, those of Urambi.
Greetings, Save, Sambiri,
The great one who observes the sacred prohibitions.
We honour you, those of the great Save River,
Mukota, the great stone-built settlements of Zimbabwe.
Indeed, we acknowledge you, Marasamas.

Notes on the translation

This is a poetic interpretation rather than a definitive literal translation. The original is a nhetembo (clan praise poem), containing ancestral names, totemic references, geographical associations and expressions of reverence that do not always translate directly into English.
Several elements are particularly significant:

1. Dziva, The Pool or Body of Water

Dziva literally means a pool, pond or deep body of water. In this poem, however, it also functions as a totemic identity (mutupo), connecting the clan to water and its associated ancestral symbolism.

2. Hove, The Fish

The line:
Dziva iri hove; hove iri nyamasase.

Can be translated provisionally as:
"The pool is the fish; the fish is nyamasase."

This establishes an intimate relationship between the body of water and the fish. I have retained nyamasase because its precise meaning in this clan-praise context needs verification.

3. Tatenda varimumvura, We Thank Those in the Water

This is one of the most evocative lines:
Tatenda varimumvura,
Dambiro ravasikana.

We give thanks to those who dwell in the water,
The bathing place of young women.

The reference to those dwelling in water may have ancestral or spiritual significance, although the poem alone does not establish precisely who is being addressed.

4. Save, The Great River

Maita zvenyu vari kuna Save rwizi rwukuru.

We honour you, those of the great Save River.

The Save (Sabi) River is an important geographical reference in Zimbabwe. Its inclusion connects the praise poem to a particular landscape and potentially to the historical movements and ancestral territories of the clan.

5. Madzimbabwe Makuru, Great Zimbabwe

Mukota, Madzimbabwe makuru.

Mukota, the great stone-built settlements of Zimbabwe.

Madzimbabwe makuru may evoke Great Zimbabwe or the wider tradition of monumental stone settlements. The specific historical reference would need confirmation.

A note on cultural interpretation

The poem is particularly interesting for its repeated associations between water, fish, ancestral identity, rivers and sacred geography.

Rather than treating these references as merely descriptive, it may be useful to understand them within the Shona tradition of nhetembo, where praise names can preserve relationships between lineages, landscapes, animals and ancestral histories.
